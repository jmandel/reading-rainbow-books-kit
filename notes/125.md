## About the book
This is a counting book that doubles as a tour of the orchestra. It opens with a single trombone playing solo. A trumpet joins in, then a French horn, and the rhyming verse keeps adding players: a trio, a quartet, a quintet, and on up to a chamber group of ten. Each instrument gets a few lines of its own, so a listener meets a cello, a clarinet and a bassoon while practicing the numbers one through ten.

The verse is full of sound words, such as the trombone's "gliding sliding" notes and the trumpet's "stings", and its rhythm carries a child along. Kirkus called it a counting book that builds "a solo becomes a duo becomes a trio" until a "complete chamber group of ten," and said Priceman's bright gouache pictures "suggest Toulouse-Lautrec in cartoon mode." Publishers Weekly praised the clever, jazzy verse and the "zany" art.

It suits toddlers and preschoolers who like counting, rhyme and sound words, and any family heading to a first concert or music class.

## Author and illustrator
Kirkus lists *Zin! Zin! Zin! a Violin* (1995) as Lloyd Moss's first book (Kirkus's review says "In his first book"), followed by two picture books: *Our Marching Band* (2001), illustrated by Diana Cain Bluthenthal, and *Music Is* (2003), illustrated by Philippe Petit-Roulet.

Marjorie Priceman was born in 1958. Wikipedia describes her books as known for bright watercolors, free-flowing lines and a whimsical spirit, and says she has illustrated more than 30 picture books.

*Zin! Zin! Zin! a Violin* was a 1996 Caldecott Honor Book. Priceman earned a second Caldecott Honor in 2006 for *Hot Air: The (Mostly) True Story of the First Hot-Air Balloon Ride*, which she wrote and illustrated.

If a young listener wants more of her pictures, Kirkus lists several she wrote and illustrated, including *How to Make an Apple Pie and See the World* (1994) and *Emeline at the Circus*.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton's lead-in, which begins mid-thought: "violins, flutes, French horns, just waiting to sing out with music." He says each instrument sounds magnificent alone, but together "something amazing happens": the sound of an orchestra. The lead-in then names the book, its author and its illustrator, and says it is read by Gregory Hines. It begins partway through a list, so the first instruments named before it were cut.

Gregory Hines then reads the whole book, from the lone trombone to the last line, "before we say a late good night." There is nothing upsetting in the audio.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar explains duos, trios and quartets, then visits a conductor rehearsing a youth orchestra, where young players talk about teamwork and practicing. LeVar then tries a percussion routine with the performers of the stage show Stomp, and young reviewers recommend three books. Finally, LeVar plays along with the orchestra.
