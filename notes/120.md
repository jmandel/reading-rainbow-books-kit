## About the book

*How Much Is a Million?* begins with a question every small child eventually asks, and answers it with pictures a toddler can follow. A mathematical magician guides the way, and the numbers climb in steps: one, ten, one hundred, one thousand, all the way to one million.

Then the book makes each number something to picture. A million kids standing on one another's shoulders would be taller than the tallest buildings. A goldfish bowl big enough for a million goldfish could hold a whale. A million tiny stars would fill seventy pages. Once a million is in hand, curious characters ask about a billion and a trillion, and each answer gets bigger than the last.

The book suits listeners who like to count, who love animals and stars, or who simply enjoy hearing very large numbers said aloud. The comparisons invite a parent to make up a few more. It was published in 1985.

## Author and illustrator

David M. Schwartz grew up in the New York area, fascinated by the biggest and smallest things in the universe, and graduated from Cornell University with a biology degree. Before writing full time he taught elementary school and worked as a journalist and a Yale Residential College Dean. He lives in northern California.

This was his first book. He has said it was inspired by a spring night spent looking at the sky, which brought back his childhood awe of space. He rewrote it twelve times, and the eighteenth publisher he tried accepted it. Children's questions led to a follow-up, *If You Made a Million*, also illustrated by Steven Kellogg. His other books include *G is for Googol* and *If You Hopped Like a Frog*.

Steven Kellogg was born in Norwalk, Connecticut, in 1941 and studied at the Rhode Island School of Design. He has created more than 90 children's books and received the Regina Medal for his lifetime contribution to children's literature. His own *Pinkerton, Behave!* led to four sequels.

## In this episode

The podcast opens with LeVar's lead-in: "One thing is for certain: a million of almost anything is a lot." He asks how you know when you've got a million, and introduces the mathematical magician who can answer. The cold open before it is cut, so the line arrives without it. The book is read by Nick Sullivan, and the audio ends on the question "Well, how much is a zillion?"

Not in the podcast audio: a visit to New York City, where a taxi driver works out how many trips a year the city's cabs make; a tour of the Crayola crayon factory; a look at how Giants Stadium estimates crowds, hot chocolate and pretzels; and children's reviews of other books.
