## About the book
A boy on his first trip to the seashore falls so hard for the waves that one of them breaks away from the sea and follows him. His father gives in, and the two sneak the wave onto a train, carried aboard "cup by small cup" and hidden in a water cooler. Once she rushes into the house, the story becomes a comic, tender tale of a very unmanageable houseguest, with light, shadows and mess in equal measure.

Kirkus Reviews called it "an outlandish and original tale" in which illustrator Mark Buehner "provides wild images," and judged that "its fresh subject and boisterous improbabilities beckon." The same review quoted a line about the wave rising "like a liquid tree" as "not a typical picture-book text" and noted that adults may read more into such lines than Cowan intends. For a young listener the appeal is plainer: a pet that is also a force of nature. The language is full of sea images, which suits children who love water and beaches.

## Author and illustrator
The story was written by the Mexican poet Octavio Paz (1914-1998), born near Mexico City and awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1990. His "My Life with the Wave" first appeared in 1951 in the prose-poem collection *¿Águila o sol?* (translated as *Eagle or Sun?*), written for adults. Catherine Cowan translated and adapted it for children in 1997. Kirkus lists her other picture-book credits as *Nikolai Gogol's The Nose* (adapted, illustrated by Kevin Hawkes) and *My Friend the Piano* (illustrated by Kevin Hawkes).

Mark Buehner, born in 1959 in Salt Lake City, Utah, is an American illustrator of children's books. He often tucks hidden images, such as rabbits, cats and dinosaurs, into his pictures, so the spreads reward a second look. Some of his books were written by his wife, Caralyn Buehner. Young listeners who enjoy his style might like *Snowmen at Night*, *Fanny's Dream* and *Superdog*, all written by Caralyn Buehner and illustrated by Mark.

## In this episode
The podcast opens on LeVar's lead-in, which begins, "Surfers know that waves are a little bit like people." He says each wave has its own personality and that waves are "definitely unpredictable," which is why the boy in this book ends up a little over his head. He then announces the title and credits.

Scott Irby-Ranniar reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line.

Content note: the wave cries and begs, smashes the boy's things, and fills the house with phantoms and monsters before the family leaves it behind.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar at Long Beach with a group of surfers; children saying what outrageous thing they would bring home; a visit to injured manatees in Florida with a park ranger and a veterinarian; scientists transplanting coral to a damaged reef; three children reviewing books; and a closing sandcastle tip.
