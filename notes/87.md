## About the book

A little pig is happily splashing in the middle of a muddy puddle when her father, her mother and her brother turn up, one after another, to tell her she is much too plump and little to be there. Each has the same remedy, lots of soap, and the piggy answers each time: "Nope." The family chants about how squishy, squashy and mushy the mud is, and the story is built almost entirely from that sound play. Then the family's plan for the stubborn piggy takes a turn.

Kirkus Reviews, in 1974, called it a playful wallow in "verbal nonsense" and praised the way James Marshall's pig family has "ludicrous gravity and blimpy buoyancy". The same review said grownups may tire of the repeated sounds. For a toddler, the repetition is the pleasure. There are words to chant along with and a refrain to wait for. It suits children who like silly rhymes, mud, and a good argument that ends in a splash.

## Author and illustrator

Charlotte Pomerantz (1930 to 2022) was an American children's writer and journalist, born in Brooklyn, New York. She graduated from Sarah Lawrence College in 1953 and died in Charlottesville, Virginia. Her book *Posy* won the Christopher Award in 1984, and her story *The Princess and the Admiral* won a Jane Addams Children's Book Award. Young listeners who enjoy this book's word play might look for her *One Duck, Another Duck* or *The Mousery*.

James Marshall (1942 to 1992) was born in San Antonio, Texas, and grew up on a family farm. He won a scholarship to study viola at the New England Conservatory, but a hand injury in an airplane accident ended his music career at 19. He earned degrees in French and history, taught French and Spanish in Boston, and then moved to New York City to work as an illustrator. He is best known for the *George and Martha* books, which began in 1972, and he illustrated *Miss Nelson Is Missing* by Harry Allard. His *Goldilocks and the Three Bears* was a Caldecott Honor book in 1989.

## In this episode

The podcast opens mid-way through the TV episode, with a line that the clay set is ready: "we've set the stage to tell a great story, but it won't come to life until we put it under the camera." Two animators then explain how a clay story is filmed, moving the characters and taking a picture, again and again. That makes the lead-in a little puzzling without the earlier pieces, which are cut.

The title and credits are read out first, and Tovah Feldshuh reads the book, voicing the piggy, the parents and the brother. The reading ends as the piggies dive down into the mud.

Not in the podcast audio: a clay-modelling opening with children, the animators building the clay puppets and puddle set, a mud-racing segment, and the children's reviews of three other books.
