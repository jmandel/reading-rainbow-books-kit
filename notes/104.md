## About the book

An old tree has grown for many years, and then a storm comes. Rain falls, thunder roars, and a lightning bolt splits the tree. A woodsman saws it down, leaving only a stump. That is where the story really begins, because the stump turns out to be a busy place. A bark beetle lays her eggs under the bark, and ants later move into the tunnels the grubs gnawed. A bear sharpens her claws on it, a titmouse perches on it, a frog shelters in one of its holes, and an earwig sleeps in its shade. The bear, the titmouse and finally a passing man each decide the stump is theirs.

The text then asks one question, "who owns the tree stump?" The sentences are short and the pace is steady, one visitor at a time, which suits a toddler who likes to spot the next animal. It also opens a quiet way to talk about forests. The book was translated from the Russian and published by Dial Books in 1985.

## Author and illustrator

Natalia Romanova wrote the text in Russian. The English edition from Dial Books is catalogued as a translation, with Gennady Spirin credited as illustrator.

Gennady Spirin was born on December 25, 1948, in Orekhovo-Zuyevo, a small town near Moscow. He graduated from the Surikov School of Fine Art in Moscow and from the Moscow Stroganov Institute of Art, and he is known for a style that joins Russian art tradition with watercolor technique. After the Soviet Union dissolved in 1992, he moved with his wife and sons to the United States, and he has lived and worked in Princeton, New Jersey, since.

His official biography says he has created more than 40 illustrated children's books, five of which won the Gold Medal from the Society of Illustrators in New York, and four of which were named a New York Times "Best Illustrated Book of the Year." He has illustrated books by Madonna and Julie Andrews. For young listeners, his illustrated alphabet rhyme *A Apple Pie* is a natural next book.

## In this episode

The podcast opens with the lead-in "In a forest, you see trees in many different stages of life, from tiny saplings like these to towering giants like this." It goes on to say that some trees fall and die, but that the life cycle of a tree does not end there. "Like these" and "like this" refer to pictures from the cut TV episode.

The book is read by Eli Wallach. The audio ends on the book's last line, "home for us all."

Content note: a lightning bolt splits an old tree, a woodsman saws it down, and the stump later rots away.

Not in the podcast audio: a scientist who studies trees from a treetop laboratory about 75 feet up; children naming things that come from trees; a visit to Vermont to see sugar maples tapped and sap boiled into syrup; the kids' book reviews; and a Harry Behn poem about trees.
