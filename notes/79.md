## About the book
*Kate Shelley and the Midnight Express* tells a true story from Iowa. Fifteen-year-old Kate Shelley is at her kitchen window with her mother and her younger sisters and brother as a terrible storm rolls in. An express train is due to pass near her house at midnight, and Kate keeps wondering how the railroad bridge over Honey Creek is holding up in the flood. Then she hears the engine's bell ring wildly and a terrible crack, and she knows she has to act.

The real events happened on July 6, 1881, when a flash flood on Honey Creek washed out a railroad trestle, according to Wikipedia's article on Kate Shelley. Rainbow Resource describes it as an exciting story with detailed color illustrations, short simple sentences and a large font, readable for younger kids. It suits listeners who like suspense and can handle some peril, and it is a good choice for children who love trains, weather or true stories about brave kids.

## Author and illustrator
Margaret K. Wetterer wrote *Kate Shelley and the Midnight Express*, published in 1990. Rainbow Resource lists the paperback as a 48-page Lerner Publications book for ages 7 to 10, in grades 1 to 3. Wikipedia's article on Kate Shelley notes that the book was featured on *Reading Rainbow*.

Karen Ritz illustrated the book. Rainbow Resource calls the pictures detailed and in color.

Wikipedia notes that the real family name was originally spelled Shelly, which is how Kate wrote her own name, before the spelling Shelley was adopted.

The real Kate Shelley later became a well-known name in Iowa railroad history. In 1901 the Chicago and North Western Railway built a steel bridge near Boone that the public nicknamed the Kate Shelley Bridge, and a second Kate Shelley Bridge, built by Union Pacific, opened on October 1, 2009, according to Wikipedia.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton on a long train ride, saying he always packs "a fast-moving, spine-tingling, hair-raising cliffhanger" to get a trip started, and introducing the book as a true story about a train in trouble. "A long train ride like this" refers to the cut opening, which the child does not hear. Brian Dennehy reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar visits the Los Angeles train yard, where railroad engineer Rosie shows him a locomotive and its cab. He boards a train for an overnight trip, and a song tells how the transcontinental railroad was built. Young readers review *The Train to Lulu's*, *The Little Engine That Could* and *The Polar Express*.

Content note: the reading includes a storm, a bridge collapse, two men clinging to branches in a flooded creek, and a frightened girl crawling across a bridge.
