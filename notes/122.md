## About the book
A little girl leaves bread on her plate, and Mamita says, "Bread is for eating." Then she sings. The song walks backward through everything that happened before the bread reached the table. First comes the seed asleep in the ground, then the sun and the rain that wake it. The farmer tills the soil, the harvester cuts the wheat, a worker takes the grain to town, and the miller grinds it into flour. The storekeeper sells the flour, and the cook and the baker finish the job. The girl answers each verse with "I'm thinking, Mamita," and her own thoughts about sprouts and the money for flour make the song hers too.

The text is short and rhythmic, with a little Spanish ("El pan is bueno"). It suits young listeners curious about where food comes from, and any child who pushes a crust to the edge of the plate.

## Author and illustrator
Phillis Gershator was born in New York City on July 8, 1942, and now lives in St. Thomas in the U.S. Virgin Islands. She studied at Berkeley and earned a B.A. from Douglass College and a library degree from Pratt Institute. She worked as a children's librarian in Brooklyn school libraries and for the St. Thomas Department of Education, and has volunteered for Reading Is Fundamental in St. Thomas since 1984. Her first book, *Honi and His Magic Circle*, appeared in 1979. In 1994 and 1995 three of her books, *Rata-pata-scata-fata*, *Tukama Tootles the Flute* and *The Iroko-Man*, won awards, and her work often draws on Caribbean and African folklore.

David Gershator, her husband since 1962, is also an author, and the two have written several picture books together. Besides *Bread Is for Eating*, which Henry Holt published in 1995, their co-written titles include *Palampam Day*, *Greetings, Sun*, *Moon Rooster* and *Kallaloo!: A Caribbean Tale*, all good next stops for young listeners.

The book was illustrated by Emma Shaw-Smith.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton in the middle of a thought: the only thing as satisfying as eating bread is making it yourself, with a little salt, some yeast and a few simple ingredients. He describes kneading ("push it, punch it, poke it"), notes that many hands besides the baker's go into a loaf, and introduces the book. The reading follows, performed by Cecilia Arana and Michael Uribe, and ends on Mamita's song and the girl's answer, "Yes, Mamita."

Not in the podcast audio, but in the television episode: children name their favorite breads; a Hopi woman from northern Arizona shows how piki, a blue cornmeal bread, is cooked on a hot stone; the owner of an Indian restaurant demonstrates naan and puri; and three child reviewers present the books listed below.
