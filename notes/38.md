## About the book

*Chickens Aren't the Only Ones* starts with a familiar fact: chickens lay the eggs you buy, boil, fry or dye. Then it turns the idea around. Every bird lays eggs, and so do snakes, lizards, crocodiles and turtles. The book moves on to frogs, fish, a mother seahorse who leaves her eggs in the father's pouch, octopuses, spiders, snails and insects, then ends its tour with two surprising mammals from Australia.

The text is rhyming verse, easy to follow by ear, with a new animal every few lines. That suits toddlers who like animal names and spotting what they know. Reviewer Carol Otis Hurst notes that most of us would answer yes when asked whether a given animal lays eggs, but would not be quick to list spiders, octopuses and sharks, and says the book "gives us egg-layers rare and common." She suggests it as an introduction to eggs or as a book for browsing. It was first published in 1981 and runs 32 pages.

## Author and illustrator

Ruth Heller (1923 to 2004) wrote and illustrated the book herself. She was born in Winnipeg, Manitoba, grew up in San Francisco, and earned a fine arts degree from the University of California, Berkeley. Her early career was in design: wrapping paper, napkins, greeting cards and coloring books. She worked mainly in colored pencil and marker.

*Chickens Aren't the Only Ones* was her first book of this kind. Penguin Random House says it was published in 1981 after five years of rejection and one full revision. She described her books as nonfiction picture books in rhyme, and felt the rhymes helped children learn facts and big words.

Listeners who enjoy this one can try her next book, *Animals Born Alive and Well* (1982), which picks up where this book leaves off with animals that do not lay eggs. Other titles include *The Reason for a Flower* (1983), *Plants That Never Ever Bloom* (1984) and the *How to Hide* series on camouflage, starting with *How to Hide a Whip-poor-will and Other Birds* (1986).

## In this episode

The podcast opens with LeVar Burton, "live on location for EGG Today," down on a farm for a mock news report. He tries to question some chickens, then asks the old riddle of whether the chicken or the egg came first, and announces a "late-breaking story," the book. The title card follows, and Georgia Engel reads the book aloud. The audio ends on the book's last line, "that's another tale to tell," after a mention of animals who have babies "born alive and well."

Not in the podcast audio: a visit to a hatchery's incubator room, where eggs stay 19 days before hatching. A song called "I'm Breaking Out," sung from a chick's point of view. A night on a Florida beach with turtle biologist Dr. Llewellyn Ehrhart, watching a loggerhead turtle lay and bury her eggs and a nest of hatchlings crawl to the sea.
