## About the book
A boy and his father travel to the Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington, D.C., to find the name of the boy's grandfather, who died in the war when Dad was young. The boy tells it in short, plain sentences as they search the rows of names, make a pencil rubbing, and notice the people around them: a veteran in a wheelchair, an older couple crying, a class of schoolgirls carrying small flags.

Kirkus praised the book's "spare, highly charged simplicity" and called it a "moving introduction" to the memorial. Publishers Weekly, recommending it for ages 4 to 8, wrote that the "well-matched text and illustrations are soft but stirring." Kirkus was less sure of two choices, questioning Himler's decision to mute the wall's black and calling the teddy bear on the title page "an unfortunate choice."

It suits families who want a quiet way to talk about a war memorial and about missing someone.

## Author and illustrator
Eve Bunting was born in Maghera, Northern Ireland, on December 19, 1928. She moved to the United States in 1958 with her husband and three children, took a writing course, and published her first story, *The Two Giants*. She wrote more than 250 books and died in 2023 at age 94. Wikipedia notes that two of her books, *Fly Away Home* and *The Wall*, were adapted as Reading Rainbow episodes.

Ronald Himler was born in Cleveland, Ohio, on November 16, 1937, and earned a diploma from the Cleveland Institute of Art in 1960 and did graduate study in painting at Cranbrook Academy of Art. He worked as a technical sculptor at General Motors until 1963, when he became an artist and illustrator, and later designed toys. He has illustrated more than a hundred books, works in watercolor, oils, gouache and pencil, and has written picture books such as *The Girl on the Yellow Giraffe* and *Six Is So Much Less than Seven*.

Bunting and Himler also made *Someday a Tree* (1993), *A Day's Work* (1994) and *Train to Somewhere* (1996).

## In this episode
LeVar Burton opens the audio standing at the memorial: "I'm standing near a wall that's very different from any wall you've ever seen." He explains that the memorial honors soldiers who died in the Vietnam War, that the wall is covered with names, and that visitors come to remember people they have lost, then introduces the book. Jason Ruggiero reads it, ending on the boy's last line, "I'd rather have him here."

Content note: the reading includes death in war, a grandfather who never came home, and adults crying at the wall.

Not in the podcast audio: an interview with Maya Lin, the architect who designed the memorial, about her choices of black granite and names listed in the order of death; a visit to the sculpture of four presidents at Mount Rushmore; a painter making his first mural, a tribute to Louis Armstrong; and children's reviews of three books.
