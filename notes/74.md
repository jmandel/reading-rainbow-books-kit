## About the book
Kondi, a boy of seven, keeps a shoebox of treasures, including a knife he made from a tin can and a dancing man made from dried cornstalks. His box also holds a few scraps of wire, saved for something special: a galimoto, a toy vehicle made of wire. His brother Ufulu laughs and says a boy of seven cannot make such a toy, because he does not have enough wire. Kondi sets off through his village to find more, trading, asking and bargaining as he goes. Along the way he meets a crowd of women at the flour mill and a small girl fishing for ants with a bit of wire.

Kirkus Reviews called it "an authentic picture of present-day Africa" and "an attractive portrayal of self-reliant children," with "good-humored bargaining and ingenuity" and "evocative double-spread watercolors." Kondi sorts, bends and shapes the wire himself, which is easy to picture. It suits children who like to build, collect and make things from whatever is around.

## Author and illustrator
Karen Lynn Williams was born in 1952 and studied at the University of Connecticut and Southern Connecticut State University. She taught the deaf in Connecticut, then taught English in Malawi as a Peace Corps teacher from 1980 to 1983. Later she lived in Haiti, where her husband worked as a doctor at the Hôpital Albert Schweitzer.

On her website, Williams says she first saw a galimoto in Africa and was intrigued by the homemade toys and the children who made them. The story began as a paragraph in an article about toys African children make. Her website lists *Galimoto* among books honored as a New York Times Book Review Top Ten Books for Children selection, the Martin Luther King "Living the Dream" Book Award and a ParentsChoice Honor Book.

Catherine Stock painted the watercolors. She also illustrated Williams's *Tap-Tap* and *Painted Dreams*, and wrote and illustrated *Where Are You Going, Manyoni?*, about a girl who walks to school across the plain, with watercolors painted in rural Zimbabwe, according to Kirkus.

## In this episode
The audio opens with LeVar Burton's lead-in: "As you can see, wire is almost everywhere." That line refers to material cut from the podcast, a talk about pipe cleaners and what is inside them, so the child hears it without that setup. He says that in this book a young African boy builds something very clever out of wire, then names the book, its author and illustrator, and the reader. The book is read aloud by Hattie Winston and runs to its last line.

Not in the podcast audio: a wire sculptor in Santa Fe, New Mexico, who shapes and paints animals such as an armadillo; a ride on the Sandia Peak Tramway in Albuquerque, where the cable that hauls the car is made of many smaller wires; a high-wire circus family; and three children reviewing books.
