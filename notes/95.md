## About the book
Long ago in Cambodia, a couple living beside a lake covered in lotus blossoms have a daughter with eyes "as bright as the stars." They name her Lotus. She cannot hear, and she never learns to speak. Her mother shapes her palms and bent fingers into a flower, and Lotus learns to name herself with her hands. She is happiest walking among the herons, cranes and white egrets, joining their graceful movements, yet she often sits alone and sad. Her worried parents decide to take her to the temple in the city, and that trip is where the story turns.

The book may suit children who love birds, music and dance, or who are curious about other ways of communicating besides talking. Kirkus calls it a "simple but pleasing story." Kirkus Reviews, in its October 1, 1991 issue, said the story was inspired by the twelfth-century temple at Angkor Wat and praised the "elegantly decorative paintings, bright with subtly contrasted color."

## Author and illustrator
Jeanne M. Lee both wrote and illustrated *Silent Lotus*, which Farrar, Straus and Giroux published on October 10, 1991, as a 32-page picture book. Kirkus describes the story as set in long-ago Kampuchea (Cambodia), where Lotus becomes a dancer at the king's palace.

Her later picture books include *The Song of Mu Lan* (1995), a translation of a sixth-century Chinese folk poem about a woman who spends more than a decade in the emperor's army disguised as a man, and *Bitter Dumplings* (2002), which Kirkus called "a tender story about kindness and trust." That one is for somewhat older listeners; Kirkus lists it for ages seven to ten. She also wrote *I Was Once a Monkey* (1999), a collection of six Buddha tales, and illustrated Laurence Yep's *The Butterfly Boy* (1993), a philosophic tale drawn from the writings of Chuang Tzu.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar's lead-in: "People are always talking, letting each other know how they think and feel." He talks about mimes and everyday gestures, then introduces *Silent Lotus*, a story of a young girl who finds a way to communicate through movement and dance.

Lea Salonga reads the book, and the audio ends on the line that Lotus became the most famous dancer in the Khmer kingdom, dancing in the king's court and in the temples of the gods.

Content note: the story includes a lonely, sad child, and her parents' worry about her.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar's reflection on deaf people's ways of communicating, a visit with a deaf friend who teaches him signs, a family and a dance class where deaf and hearing kids dance together, and the kids' reviews of three books.
