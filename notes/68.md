## About the book
*The Bicycle Man* looks back on one spring day at a small Japanese school, halfway up a tall green mountain with a view of the town, the harbor and the sea. Kirkus Reviews describes it as "reportedly a memory from Say's childhood," set at a school sports day in 1946. The children wear red headbands, parents bring lunch boxes and tea, and prizes come wrapped in white paper and gold thread. Then two strangers appear at the fence. They are American soldiers, the first Americans the children have ever seen, and the narrator admits he felt afraid. One soldier soon asks about the principal's bicycle, and the day takes an unexpected turn.

Kirkus called it "A delightful story," praised the "charming little figures" in the pictures, and added, "Say makes no comments and none are needed." The story moves through races, cheering and giggling, and builds to a crowd watching something new.

## Author and illustrator
Allen Say wrote and illustrated this book. He was born James Allen Koichi Moriwaki Seii on August 28, 1937, in Yokohama, Japan. He apprenticed to the cartoonist Noro Shinpei, whom he called his "spiritual father." Later he worked as a sign painter and a photographer, and during his Army service in Germany his photographs were published in *Stars and Stripes*.

Houghton Mifflin approached him with a retelling of a Japanese folktale, *The Boy of the Three-Year Nap*, which won the Boston Globe-Horn Book Award in 1988 and was named a Caldecott Honor book in 1989. He illustrated *How My Parents Learned to Eat*, which received a Christopher Award in 1985. *Grandfather's Journey*, which he wrote and illustrated, won the Caldecott Medal in 1994. His memoir *Drawing from Memory* won an Oregon Book Award in 2013. Say has lived in Portland, Oregon, since 1999.

Young listeners who enjoy this story may like *Grandfather's Journey* or *The Boy of the Three-Year Nap* next.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with the host talking about wheels: "It doesn't matter what you're riding as long as you're moving on a fast set of wheels." He describes rollerblades, says wheels have "kept civilization rolling along," and introduces bicycles that people ride all over the world. Then comes the book, read aloud by Tom Matsusaka. The audio ends as the children follow the Americans with their eyes until they disappear around the bend.

Content note: the narrator says he felt afraid when the strangers appeared, and the children draw back before they begin to laugh.

Not in the podcast audio:
- A visit with a freestyle bicyclist who explains the modified bike he uses for tricks.
- A look at human-powered vehicles built for speed.
- A skateboarding lesson that names the deck, trucks and grip tape.
- Young reviewers' recommendations of the books below.
