## About the book

In a windswept place "near nowhere and close to forgotten," an old man lives in a small house surrounded by other people's garbage. Every day he sorts, burns and buries the junk, and every night he dreams of a jungle full of toucans, tree frogs and tigers. One day an idea takes root, and out of the scrap he builds a forest of tin trees and tin animals. It is not the forest of his dreams, but it is a forest. Then a small bird blows in on the wind, and the story turns.

Kirkus Reviews called it a parable and noted Wayne Anderson's dingy blues for the junk against brighter colors for living things. Publishers Weekly wrote that "exquisitely detailed illustrations endow this touching tale with pathos and grace." The text is short and rhythmic, which suits young listeners, though reviewers pitched it a little older: ages 6 to 8 (Kirkus) and 6 to 10 (Publishers Weekly). It is a quiet story about patience, hope and caring for a place.

## Author and illustrator

Helen Ward is a British author and illustrator who lives in Stroud, Gloucestershire. She trained at Brighton School of Art, and in 1985, her final year there, she won the first Walker Prize for Children's Illustration. Her retellings of *The Hare and the Tortoise* and *The Wind in the Willows* won National Art Library Awards, and *The Tin Forest* won the children's trade category at the British Book Design and Production Awards in 2003. A natural next book is her *Varmints*, illustrated by Marc Craste, another story in which nature is crowded out until something small changes things.

Wayne Anderson enrolled at Leicester College of Art in 1961, at fifteen, and started his illustration career in London. Since his first color commission in 1969 he has done nearly all his color work in colored pencil. He is known for fantasy art, especially dragons: he illustrated Peter Dickinson's *The Flight of Dragons* (1979), and has written or illustrated more than 160 books.

## In this episode

LeVar opens at Ground Zero in lower Manhattan, a year after September 11, 2001. In the podcast audio he says plainly that two planes crashed into the World Trade Center towers and that the buildings collapsed, then describes rescue teams and a neighborhood rebuilding with hope. Parents of very young listeners may want to hear this lead-in first. He then introduces the book as a story about hope in a dark and gloomy place. Jeff Bridges reads the book.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar visits PS 234, an elementary school near the site whose students spent months at another school before returning. The children talk about that day, show gifts sent from around the world (including 10,000 paper cranes from Japan), build a world-map mural, photograph the firefighters and shopkeepers who helped them, and record a thank-you song, "Back Where We Belong."
