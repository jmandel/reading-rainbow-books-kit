## About the book
A New York mailman named Mr. Tim has a certified letter for a man called John Peck, and Peck is never where the letter expects him. When the letter reaches Boise, Idaho, Peck has just left for Zurich. When it reaches Zurich, he has gone off to Brazil. The poem follows the letter by train, plane and ship, past clerks sorting mail through the night.

Marshak's rhyming verse suits reading aloud, and the place names, from Schenectady to Skokie, are fun to hear. It is a good match for a child who waits at the door for the mail or asks where a letter goes after it is dropped in the box.

*Publishers Weekly* (July 1, 1990) praised Richard Pevear's translation of the verse for its wit and gaiety, and said Vladimir Radunsky's illustrations "steal the show." It recommended the book for ages 4 to 7. Henry Holt published the book in 1990.

## Author and illustrator
Samuil Marshak (1887-1964) was born in Voronezh, Russia, and studied at the University of London. Wikipedia calls him a Soviet writer, translator and poet who wrote for both children and adults. In the 1920s his children's books were published by Raduga, whose name means "rainbow" in English. His honors include four Stalin Prizes and the Lenin Prize in 1963. Henry Holt also published his *The Pup Grew Up* in 1989.

Vladimir Radunsky (1954-2018) was born in Perm, grew up in Moscow, and moved to New York in 1982, where he worked as a graphic designer and made art books and children's books. His books are playful in form: *Square Triangle Round Skinny* is a set of four books shaped the way their titles suggest. He illustrated Chris Raschka's *Hip-Hop Dog* and made *The Mighty Asparagus*, which Wikipedia lists with a New York Times Best Illustrated Books award.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar's lead-in: "Hi, there's nothing I like better than opening my mailbox and finding it filled with letters." He sorts through his own mail, tosses a junk letter into the recycling, and hears children say what they get in the mail, including pen pal letters, a Valentine card and a coconut in a box. A delivery from his friend Phil brings the book, and LeVar announces it.

Al Roker reads the book, and the audio ends on the book's last line, "hail to their heavy bags that bring the mail."

Not in the podcast audio: children read out letters they wrote, from "Dear Grandma" to "Dear Mr. President"; a look inside a post office where machines sort letters; a comic history of early mail carriers; and a 12-year-old stamp collector showing his collection. Children then recommend three books.
