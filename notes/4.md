## About the book
*Bringing the Rain to Kapiti Plain* is a Nandi tale from Kenya, retold in rhyme by Verna Aardema and published by Dial in 1981. It opens on Kapiti Plain, green and full of animals in the rains, then turns to a year when the rains are very late and the grass turns brown and dead. A herd boy named Ki-pat stands on one leg like a stork, watching his hungry cows, and an eagle drops a feather near him.

The telling is cumulative. Each page adds one more object to a growing chain and repeats everything before it, in the manner of "This Is the House That Jack Built." Kirkus said that this borrowed form "gives this version a brisk, easy lilt." Kirkus adds that "the Africans never told it this way to be sure," since the English rhyme form is borrowed.

It suits young listeners who like rhythm, refrains and animals, and it fits a rainy afternoon.

## Author and illustrator
Verna Aardema (1911-2000) was born Verna Norberg in New Era, Michigan, and earned a journalism degree from Michigan State in 1934. According to Wikipedia, she began writing children's stories because her daughter would eat only if she was read a story. Her first collection, *Tales from the Story Hat*, came out in 1960, and she went on to retell folktales, mostly African and Mexican. *Why Mosquitoes Buzz in People's Ears* (1975), illustrated by Leo and Diane Dillon, received the Caldecott Medal in 1976.

Beatriz Vidal grew up in Argentina, where her father's stories and poems fed her interest in legends and folktales. She moved to New York and studied painting and design with the Hungarian artist Ilonka Karasz. Her publisher's biography lists *Rainbow Crow*, *The Legend of El Dorado*, *A Library for Juana* and *Federico and the Magi's Gift* among her best-known books; she both wrote and illustrated *Federico and the Magi's Gift*. She has taught illustration workshops in Argentina, Cyprus, China and Mongolia.

## In this episode
The podcast begins with LeVar Burton, on a rainy day, settling in with a cool glass of apple juice and a good book, "or two or 10." He calls this one of his favorite stories, based on an old African legend told by the Nandi people of Kenya, and a perfect rainy-day story. He asks the listener to imagine a dry, thirsty land with no rain for weeks. The recording goes straight into the book, read by James Earl Jones, and ends on its last line, about the cloud heavy with rain over Kapiti Plain.

Content note: the lead-in mentions an empty stomach during a long drought.

Not in the podcast audio: the rainy-day opening with a forecast and a puddle-hopping song. After the book come LeVar's childhood thunder story, children's ideas about thunder, a visit with scientists studying thunderstorms from an airplane and by radar, weather folklore, children's book reviews, and a rainbow made with a glass of water.
