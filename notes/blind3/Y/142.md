## About the book

*The Tin Forest* begins in "a wide windswept place near nowhere," a dumping ground where a lonely old man sifts through garbage every day. At night he dreams of a forest full of birds, flowers and tigers. By day he builds one of his own from scrap: "A forest made of garbage. A forest made of tin." Its first turn comes when a small bird perches in the tin branches and then vanishes, leaving the old man's heart aching with emptiness.

Reviewers praise the art more than the text. *Publishers Weekly* says "Exquisitely detailed illustrations endow this touching tale with pathos and grace." Kirkus, which calls the book "a parable," says Wayne Anderson "faithfully portrays every leaf and vine, every nick and rivet." Kirkus is less enthusiastic about the story, finding it "Less developed both in art and storyline" than Colin Thompson's *Paper Bag Prince*, though it adds that the book "may still furnish thoughtful readers with indirect motivation to act on their hearts' desires." *Publishers Weekly* calls its message "all the more inspiring for its understatement."

## Author and illustrator

Helen Ward wrote *The Tin Forest* and Wayne Anderson illustrated it. Penguin Random House's author page says the two "have worked together on a number of splendid picture books" and live in England. Booklist, quoted on the same page, calls their work "infused with natural wonder," "beautifully illustrated," and "stunning." Bookroo credits Ward as author of five picture books and as illustrator of two, and lists *Old Shell, New Shell* (2001), *The Rooster and the Fox* (2002), *The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse* (2011) and *Spots in a Box* (2015) alongside *The Tin Forest*. Bookroo also credits Anderson as the illustrator of *The Tin Forest* (2001). Those four titles are the next place to look for a listener who enjoyed the book's quiet, patient pacing.

## In this episode

The lead-in is the host's line "Here's a book that's all about the message of hope." The next sentence describes a man in "a dark and gloomy place, which is how this neighborhood seemed right after the tragedy." Those words refer to lower Manhattan after the September 11, 2001 attacks, shown in the cut opening, so the child hears "this neighborhood" and "the tragedy" with no explanation. Jeff Bridges reads the book. The audio ends on the book's last line, "who never stopped dreaming."

Not in the podcast audio: an on-location opening at ground zero, and a later segment at PS 234 in which students describe the day of the attacks and the gifts they received from around the world, followed by a music video project.

Content note: the lead-in mentions "the tragedy" without saying what it was.
