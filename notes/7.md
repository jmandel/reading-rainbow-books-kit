## About the book
Liang is a poor boy in long-ago China who wants to paint more than anything but cannot afford a brush. A teacher turns him away, so he draws birds in the sand with a twig until an old man appears and puts a magic brush in his hand: whatever Liang paints comes to life. He paints toys for children, and when he sells pictures he leaves something out so they will not fly away. Then a greedy emperor hears about the brush. The tale is short and easy to follow, full of things to picture, and suits listeners who enjoy a folktale in which a clever, kind person outwits someone powerful.

Reviews differ. Publishers Weekly (1988) called it "a masterful story that glows with Demi's jewel-like art," and Booklist said her telling "moves gracefully in keeping with fragile lines and gentle touches of color." Kirkus (1980) was negative, calling the tale "mundanely told" with "faint, finicky, minute pictures" and ending "Pallid to the last."

## Author and illustrator
Demi (born Charlotte Dumaresq Hunt, September 2, 1942, in Cambridge, Massachusetts) writes and illustrates her own books. Her father gave her the nickname when he first saw his small baby daughter. Her mother was an artist. She earned a B.A. at Immaculate Heart College in Los Angeles, where she studied under Sister Corita, then a master's degree at the University of Baroda in India, where she was a Fulbright scholar.

She painted murals and exhibited as a painter before turning to children's books in the late 1970s; her first picture book, *Lu Pan, the Carpenter's Apprentice*, appeared in 1978. Encyclopedia.com lists *Liang and the Magic Paintbrush* among her honors as an American Library Association Notable Book selection in 1980. Wikipedia credits her with more than 300 titles. Young listeners who like this one might try her *The Empty Pot* (1990), *Chen Ping and His Magic Axe*, or *One Grain of Rice*.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton's lead-in: "Making something come to life is part of the magic of being an artist of any kind." He asks what would happen if everything you drew came to life, then introduces a boy who wants to be an artist. That line follows a Chinatown lion dance that is cut, so "come to life" arrives without context. Lauren Tom reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line, "painting for the poor, wherever he went."

Not in the podcast audio: the Chinatown lion dance; a calligrapher and a seal carver; a computer graphics lab where an artist paints the story's dragon boat; a restaurant meal; and three young reviewers.

Content note: the greedy emperor has Liang bound and imprisoned, the emperor's painted tree becomes a python that nearly bites his head off, and his boat breaks apart so that he and the royal family sink to the bottom of the sea.
