## About the book

On a summer night in 1939, eight-year-old Cassie Louise Lightfoot lies on the roof of her family's Harlem apartment building, which she calls Tar Beach. Her parents play cards with the neighbors, her little brother lies beside her, and the George Washington Bridge glitters nearby. Then the stars lift Cassie up, and she flies over the city, claiming the bridge and the buildings she loves as her own.

Publishers Weekly said Ringgold's "strong figures and flattened perspective bring a distinctive magic" to the book. Kirkus Reviews closed its notice by calling it "Beautiful, innovative, and full of the joy of one unconquerable soul." Penguin Random House lists it for ages 5 to 8.

It suits children who like to imagine and who enjoy a story told in one child's voice. The flight at its heart is a child's imagination claiming the whole city.

## Author and illustrator

Faith Ringgold wrote and illustrated the book herself. Born in Harlem in 1930, she earned degrees from City College of New York, taught in the New York City public schools, and left in 1973 to make art full-time. She became best known for painted story quilts, combining painting, quilting and storytelling.

Tar Beach began as a story quilt of the same name, made in 1988. In 1989 an editor, Andrea Cascardi, saw a poster of the quilt in a colleague's office and tracked Ringgold down through her gallery. She said the quilt "came from a memory from my childhood," of going up to the roof with her family to see the George Washington Bridge. The quilt now hangs in the Guggenheim Museum. This was Ringgold's first picture book. It was a Caldecott Honor Book, won the Coretta Scott King Award for illustration, and received the Ezra Jack Keats New Writer Award.

Listeners who like Cassie may enjoy Ringgold's *Aunt Harriet's Underground Railroad in the Sky*, *If a Bus Could Talk: The Story of Rosa Parks* and *Cassie's Word Quilt*.

## In this episode

The podcast opens on the rooftop, with LeVar Burton's lead-in: "Today, we're in an extraordinary place. It's a rooftop high above New York City." He explains that flat city roofs are painted with black tar, which is why people call them tar beaches, and he points out water towers, tiny-looking streets and the George Washington Bridge. He introduces Cassie's story. Ruby Dee reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line, about flying among the stars.

Content note: in the story, Cassie's father cannot join the union, and the book uses the words "colored" and "half-breed Indian" in describing how others label him. Cassie imagines that her mother will no longer cry all winter when he goes looking for work.

Not in the podcast audio: a segment on how the George Washington Bridge holds up its roadway, with bridge workers talking about their jobs; a rooftop gardener and a pigeon keeper; and child reviewers recommending books.
