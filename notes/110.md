## About the book

On November 19, 1916, aviator Ruth Law set out to fly from Chicago to New York City in a single day, something nobody had done before. She sleeps in a tent on a roof to get used to the cold, layers wool and leather suits under a skirt, and flies a small, old plane with an open cockpit. Mechanics add a second gas tank, then take off the lights to save weight, so she must finish before dark.

It suits children who love airplanes, true adventures and stories about real women who did something first. The text is short and the pace quick, with a few funny details, such as the maps taped together into a scroll on her leg.

Kirkus Reviews, in 1993, praised the "easy, concise style spiced with amusing details" and called the sketchy pen and watercolor illustrations a good fit for "the period, the vast sky, and Law's remarkably frail-looking craft." Its verdict: "A likable story, well worth telling."

## Author and illustrator

Don Brown wrote and illustrated this book, so one person is both. He was born in 1949 and studied history. Before writing for children he worked as a movie theatre manager, clam digger, waiter, cook and bartender, and from around 1980 he worked as a freelance illustrator and animator. He is married, has two daughters, and gives his home as Merrick, New York.

The book came about while he searched library shelves for his daughters, hoping to find inspiring stories of women's contributions to history. He found none, so he decided to write them himself. *Ruth Law Thrills a Nation*, published in 1993, was his first book and launched his career. It was named a Notable Trade Book in the Field of Social Studies by the National Council for the Social Studies and the Children's Book Council in 1993.

Young listeners who enjoy this one may like more of his picture-book biographies, including *Alice Ramsey's Grand Adventure* (1997), *One Giant Leap: The Story of Neil Armstrong* (1998) and *Rare Treasure: Mary Anning and Her Remarkable Discoveries* (1999).

## In this episode

The audio opens with the lead-in, which begins, "Isn't this a beauty? It's an old-fashioned biplane." It explains how an open-air biplane works, from the control stick to the big rubber tires, and says early pilots were brave, some of them daredevils. It ends by introducing "one of those pilots," Ruth Law.

Linda Lavin reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line, that Ruth Law set an American non-stop cross-country flying record, 590 miles, and thrilled a nation.

Content note: the reading includes a fierce wind shaking the plane, onlookers watching in fear, and an engine quitting from an empty fuel tank before a forced landing.

Not in the podcast audio: a short history of women pilots (Bessie Coleman, Amelia Earhart, Jackie Cochran and women astronauts), a profile of a 17-year-old New York high school student learning to fly and flying solo, and the kids' book reviews.
