## About the book
Oobie-do John is a cool cat who lives alone in a riverside shack and wants only one thing, to make jazzy music on his sax. He hops the night train to a city by a bay, lands in a club called Minnie's Can Do, and the crowd goes wild. Fame does not pay the rent, though. Minnie can offer only peanuts, the other jazz joints belong to the top dogs, and a cat who wants to make it has to keep trying.

The text is a stream of rhymes, scat sounds and slang that begs to be read aloud, suited to listeners who like to bounce and chant along. *Kirkus Reviews* called the story "rhythmic rap" and praised the pictures, saying Woodleigh Hubbard's "vibrant colors and expressive figures" are the strongest feature of the book. The same review judged it "neither as witty nor as original" as Chris Raschka's *Charlie Parker Played Be Bop*. Chronicle Books published it in 1993.

## Author and illustrator
Jonathan London was born in Brooklyn, New York, on March 11, 1947. He studied history and social sciences at San Jose State University, where he earned a master's degree, and in college he became a poet. Afterward he joined a dance company in San Francisco, wrote poetry and short fiction, and traveled the world. He published around 200 poems and stories in literary magazines but did not make a living from children's books until he was 45. His best-known work is the Froggy series, which includes the bestseller *Froggy Gets Dressed*; more than 16 million Froggy books have sold in North America. He has published over 120 books, 30 of them Froggy titles.

Woodleigh Marx Hubbard illustrated *Hip Cat*, and she and London teamed up again for *Park Beat: Rhymin' Through the Seasons* (HarperCollins, 2001), a rhymed picture book about a park through the four seasons for ages 2 to 5. Her other illustrated books include *The Precious Gift*, written by Ellen Jackson (1996), and *Imaginary Menagerie*, written by Layne Longfellow (1997).

## In this episode
The podcast opens with the lead-in: "When you improvise, you let your feelings tell you what to do. Here's a book about a jazz cat who did just that." It refers to a lesson the podcast skips, so a child hears the word "improvise" without the setup. Ann Duquesnay reads the book, and the audio ends on the line "Oobie-do John, the sax man, scat man." Content note: the cat is short of money, lives in a tent for a while and feels blue, all briefly.

Not in the podcast audio: the TV episode opens with a band rehearsing jazz on a city corner and shows improvising in storytelling, painting and dancing. After the book, saxophonist Joshua Redman talks about teaching himself from records, and visits a shop that repairs saxophones. A club set follows, then three kids review books.
