## About the book
*The Gift of the Sacred Dog* opens with a hungry Plains camp. The people have walked for many days looking for buffalo, the dogs are too tired to drag their loads, and even the wolves are calling out with hunger. The wise men say the people must dance. A boy decides to climb the highest hill alone and ask the Great Spirit for help. On the hilltop, storm clouds close in and thunderbirds swoop overhead, and he is frightened before anything answers him.

The book retells a Plains legend about the first horses, which the people called sacred dogs. Goble's introductory note says that to the nomadic buffalo hunters "horses were truly miraculous." Kirkus called his telling "plain and undramatic," and found the illustrations of "fresh ... attractiveness," though they "tend to become slick and craftsy at peak moments." The plain telling suits reading aloud: short sentences, a clear journey up a hill, and a slow build of wind, hail and thunder. Children who like horses, weather and quiet legends will find the most here.

## Author and illustrator
Paul Goble wrote and illustrated the book himself. Born in Haslemere, England, in 1933, he grew up in Oxford, where his father made harpsichords and his mother was a professional musician. He studied at the Central School of Art in London and worked as an art teacher and furniture designer before his first children's book, *Red Hawk's Account of Custer's Last Battle*, came out in 1969.

Publishers Weekly's obituary says his mother read him Native American tales as a boy, and that he collected pictures of Plains peoples. The summer after graduating from art school he toured Sioux and Crow reservations. By 1977 he had published four picture books, and he moved to the Black Hills of South Dakota to write and paint full time. His book *The Girl Who Loved Wild Horses* won the Caldecott Medal in 1979. He became a U.S. citizen in 1984 and died in Rapid City in January 2017, aged 83.

Listeners who enjoy this book may like his *The Girl Who Loved Wild Horses*, *Buffalo Woman* (1984) and *Crow Chief* (1992).

## In this episode
LeVar Burton opens on the plains, saying many tribes lived there for thousands of years and that most call themselves "the people." He explains that horses let people hunt and travel faster, often "the difference between hunger and plenty," then introduces the book. Michael Ansara reads the full story, and the audio ends on the line about thanking the spirits of the buffalo.

Content note: the story includes hunger and suffering, and the boy is afraid during the storm.

Not in the podcast audio: horse poems, then a visit to the Crow Fair in Crow Agency, Montana, with the Old Elk family; a segment on Native American names and place names, with children choosing names for themselves; and three child reviews of *Moonsong Lullaby*, *Why Mosquitoes Buzz in People's Ears* and *Suho and the White Horse*.
