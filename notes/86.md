## About the book

*Come a Tide* is set in the Appalachian mountains, where a family watches snow turn to rain that falls for four days and nights. Grandma says there is going to be a flood, and she is right. The creeks fill, the river rises, and the narrator's family sees gardens, pigs, chickens and even a porch float past the bridge. Kirkus describes the water running down "like kinfolks coming home." Then a whistle sounds in the night, and neighbors must decide whether to leave. One of them will not leave until he has found his duck.

Publishers Weekly called it "a gem of a picture book" and noted that the text captures country speech without condescension. Kirkus singled out the illustrations, with their rainbow colors streaked by the downpour and the amusing details in the debris.

The book suits children of about four to seven, as Publishers Weekly lists it, and for anyone who likes stories of neighbors helping each other. It is a warm retelling of a frightening night, with humor throughout.

## Author and illustrator

George Ella Lyon was born in 1949 in Harlan, Kentucky, a coal mining town. She studied at Centre College, the University of Arkansas and Indiana University, and taught writing at several colleges. Her first collection, the poetry book *Mountain*, appeared in 1983. Her work, in picture books, poetry and novels, often centers on Appalachia. She won the Golden Kite Award in 1989 for *Borrowed Children* and later served as Kentucky Poet Laureate. Other picture books include *Mama Is a Miner*, *A Day at Damp Camp*, *All the Water in the World* and *Trains Run!*.

Stephen Gammell, born in 1943, grew up in Iowa and is self-taught. He has illustrated nearly 70 books. He won the Caldecott Medal for *Song and Dance Man* by Karen Ackerman, and Caldecott Honors for *Where the Buffaloes Begin* and for *The Relatives Came* by Cynthia Rylant, a book also set in the Appalachian hills. He also illustrated two other Lyon picture books: *A Regular Rolling Noah* and, years later, *My Friend, the Starfinder*.

## In this episode

The podcast opens with a lead-in about clouds: "You know, I never get tired of looking at the clouds." It goes on to shapes in the sky, cirrus, stratus and cumulus clouds, and how rain can be a gift but sometimes brings too much water, which leads straight into the title card. Dixie Carter then reads the whole book, and the audio ends on the grandmother's line, "Kids, there's a flood coming."

Content note: the reading includes a night flood warning, a family leaving home in a hurry, floodwater in the house and a lot of cleanup.

Not in the podcast audio: the rest of the television episode, including tornado and hail footage, a snowstorm, lightning, satellite weather maps, a report on Hurricane Hugo reaching Charleston, South Carolina in September 1989 and the rebuilding afterward, a homemade rain gauge, and children reviewing books.
