## About the book
A boy who lives at 165 East 95th Street in New York City is sure he is going to stay there forever. Then his parents announce a move out west. He is certain everyone out west is a sheriff, that he will have to ride a horse to school, and that he will eat chili and beans three times a day, far from his best friend Seymour and their salami sandwiches. Seymour has also warned him that Gila monsters meet you at the airport.

At the airport he meets a boy heading the other way, with tall tales of his own about the East. The boy's picture of the West and the picture the other boy has of the East turn out to be tall tales traded between two travelers.

It suits children who are moving, starting somewhere new, or worried about the unknown. The refrain "I read it in a book, so I know it, so" invites a talk about what is true and what is only imagined.

## Author and illustrator
Marjorie Weinman Sharmat (1928-2019) was born in Portland, Maine, and graduated from Westbrook Junior College in 1948. Her picture book *Rex*, illustrated by Emily McCully, appeared in 1967. She went on to write more than 130 books for children and young adults. Her best-known creation is the boy detective Nate the Great, who first appeared in 1972 and was named after her father. Younger listeners may also like her picture book *Mitchell Is Moving* (1978), illustrated by Jose Aruego and Ariane Dewey.

Byron Barton (1930-2023) was born Byron Vartanian in Pawtucket, Rhode Island, and moved to New York after art school, working as an ad designer and animator. Publishers Weekly described his picture books for very young children as combining simple text with bold blocks of color and thick black outlines. His own titles include *Airport* (1982), *Building a House* (1981), *I Want to Be an Astronaut* (1988) and *Dinosaurs, Dinosaurs* (1989) and the *My Car* and *My Bus* books.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton standing outside 165 East 95th Street in New York, where boxes are being carried into a moving van. He talks about the jittery feeling of moving, changing classrooms or leaving friends, then introduces the book. He says the book's boy once lived in "this very building," which refers to that street scene. The book is read by Douglas Parvin and ends on the line "Back east, they don't know much about us westerners."

Content note: the boy imagines frightening things about the West and misses his best friend.

Not in the podcast audio: a second-grade memory from LeVar, children guessing what a Gila monster looks like, and a visit to the Arizona desert with a biologist from the Arizona Game and Fish Department. She shows a real Gila monster, a saguaro cactus, and animal tracks by a river. Also cut are a turtle song, children talking about moving, and three young reviewers.
