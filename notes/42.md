## About the book
A poor miller boasts to the king that his beautiful daughter can spin straw into gold. The king has a passion for gold, so he locks the girl in a room full of straw and warns her that it must be gold by morning. When she begins to weep, a tiny man steps through the door and offers to do the spinning for a price. Each night the room is bigger and the price is higher.

Paul O. Zelinsky retold the tale after, in Kirkus Reviews' words, "comparing several of the original Grimm variants," and Kirkus called the result "graceful and lucid." The same review described the paintings as "opulently painted, full of classical architectural detail, fantastic distant landscapes," and called the imp "a bug-eyed, spindle-legged Machiavelli," dressed as a courtier. Kirkus closed by calling it "a distinguished edition of one of Grimm's favorite tales."

It suits listeners who enjoy a classic fairy tale with a name-guessing game. The Gathering Books blog found the "Renaissance-like illustrations" gave the well-loved tale "a fresh voice and a new feel/look."

## Author and illustrator
The tale comes from the Brothers Grimm, who first called it "Rumpenstunzchen." Zelinsky's author's note says the story changed across the Grimms' editions and names the second edition of 1819 as closest to the version most people know.

Paul O. Zelinsky was born in Evanston, Illinois, and grew up in Wilmette. He drew constantly as a child; at four he sent work to Highlights magazine. At Yale he took Maurice Sendak's class on the history and art of children's books, which pointed him toward a career in the field. He then studied at the Tyler School of Art graduate school in Philadelphia and Rome, and Renaissance and Italian art have fascinated him ever since. His book illustrating career began in 1978 with Avi's *Emily Upham's Revenge*.

*Rumpelstiltskin* was a 1987 Caldecott Honor book. Zelinsky also received Honors for *Hansel and Gretel* (1985) and *Swamp Angel* (1995), and won the 1998 Caldecott Medal for *Rapunzel*. Listeners may also like his movable book *The Wheels on the Bus*.

## In this episode
The episode opens at the Renaissance Pleasure Faire in Agoura Hills, California. The podcast audio begins with LeVar joking that being a knight in shining armor felt like "rattling around in an old tin can," then showing a spinning wheel and explaining how it turns fiber into yarn. He introduces the tale as a famous fairy tale about a young girl whose spinning wheel turned straw into gold. The earlier fair scenes are cut, so the armor joke comes without context.

Ralph Waite reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line, "And he was never heard from again."

Content note: the audio includes a king's threat that the girl will die, her fear and weeping, and a promise to give up her first child.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar learns shearing, carding, dyeing and weaving from a costumed spinner, Mistress Nora, then watches jugglers and joins a feast. Three young reviewers then recommend other books.
