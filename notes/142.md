## About the book
An old man lives in a small house in a wide, windswept place filled with things nobody wanted. Each day he sifts, sorts, burns and buries the garbage, and each night he dreams of a forest full of wild animals, with toucans, tree frogs and tigers. Each morning the world outside is unchanged, until one day something catches his eye and an idea takes root.

The story is told in short, calm sentences, which suits a young listener, and it leaves room to talk about dreams, patience and what can be made from what is thrown away. Kirkus praised the pictures, writing that Wayne Anderson "faithfully portrays every leaf and vine, every nick and rivet," and noted that the "brief, large-type text in a soft gray" mimics the drab forest at first. Kirkus also judged the book less developed in art and story than Colin Thompson's *Paper Bag Prince*, but said it may still "furnish thoughtful readers with indirect motivation to act on their hearts' desires."

## Author and illustrator
Helen Ward wrote *The Tin Forest*, which Dutton published in the United States on September 1, 2001. Kirkus lists her as author of other picture books, among them *The Dragon Machine* (2003) and *Little Moon Dog* (2007), both illustrated by Wayne Anderson. She has also adapted and illustrated stories herself, including *The Rooster and the Fox* (2003) and *The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse* (2012), and illustrated *The Animals' Christmas Carol* (2001).

Wayne Anderson illustrated *The Tin Forest* and has a long list of picture books. Kirkus lists *Thumbelina*, adapted by James Riordan (1991), *Dragon* (1992), which he both wrote and illustrated, and *Dragons* (1993). He also illustrated *Faucet Fish* by Fay Robinson (2005) and *The Squirrel Wife* by Philippa Pearce (2007).

Kirkus found the "softly textured illustrations" of Anderson's *Dragon Machine* sweet, with "dreamlike shifts of scale," though it called the story lightweight, and described the fairies and landscapes of *Little Moon Dog* as "rendered with Rackham-esque depth." Listeners who like this book's pictures may enjoy those two next.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton's lead-in: "Here's a book that's all about the message of hope." The lead-in refers to cut material, since "this neighborhood" and "the tragedy" were explained in a segment filmed at ground zero in New York City after September 11, 2001. A child hears them without context. The book is read by Jeff Bridges, who introduces it with the credits "Written by Helen Ward. Illustrated by Wayne Anderson. And read by me, Jeff Bridges." The audio ends on the last line, "And in the house lived an old man who never stopped dreaming."

Content note: the lead-in mentions a tragedy, and the story includes an old man whose heart aches with emptiness.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar's closing remarks on hope, a visit with students at PS 234 who returned to their school after the attacks, their thank-you music video and map mural, and the students' own recommendations of books.
