## About the book
A girl finds two old potatoes in a cupboard at her dad's house, sprouts growing from their eyes, and throws them out. Her dad says to wait: he thinks they can grow new potatoes. After a call to Grandpa, the two dig a bed in the sunniest spot in the garden, cut the potatoes into pieces, and plant them. The story follows the growing season from May to September, with weeding, watering, compost and a worrying stretch when the plants turn brown. Small talk between father and daughter shows the family has recently changed.

It suits young gardeners; the counting chant near the end ("One potato, two potato, three potato, four") invites joining in. Penguin Random House says it covers the basic steps of planting potatoes while quietly addressing the parents' divorce. Kirkus found the "colloquial and casual and just right" narration and the "optimism and good cheer" of the book appealing, but called Fisher's illustrations "anything but casual," noting that they work the text into the design so that the letters are often hard to read.

## Author and illustrator
John Coy writes books for children and young adults and lives in Minneapolis. Penguin Random House notes that his *Night Driving* was a Horn Book Fanfare Honor Book and that *Strong to the Hoop* was an ALA Notable Children's Book. Both are picture books a young listener might enjoy next, along with *Vroomaloom Zoom*. According to Wikipedia, *Night Driving* (1996) was his first picture book and drew on family road trips, and *Strong to the Hoop* connected him with the NBA's Read to Achieve program. He grew up the eldest of four children of educators.

Carolyn Fisher is an illustrator and author of books for young readers who lives in Calgary, Canada. Her illustrations have earned honors from the American Library Association and the Society of Illustrators of Los Angeles. She has also written and illustrated *The Snow Show* and *A Twisted Tale*, both good places to look for more of her work. Kirkus lists *Two Old Potatoes and Me* as a 2003 Knopf picture book.

## In this episode
The podcast opens inside the TV episode's on-location start: a father and his daughter Michaela toss tea bags, banana peels and a moldy lemon onto a compost pile, explaining that scraps rot into soil that is "great for your garden." The host then says, "Just because you can't use something the same way you did before doesn't mean you have to throw it out," and introduces the book as a story about an old potato. Jordan Puryear reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line.

Content note: the girl and her dad talk about her bedroom at her mom's house and her Friday pickup.

Not in the podcast audio: a segment on plastic made from corn, a New York shop selling recycled goods, an artist who turns a broken fan into a clock, a maker who molds old vinyl records into bowls, three kids reviewing books, and a father and daughter mashing potatoes.
