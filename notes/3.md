## About the book
Bea Jones has had it with kindergarten. She is tired of bean bag games, clothespin games and the dumb colored lollipop game, and over breakfast she tells her father so. He, as it happens, is tired of running for the 7:45 train and laughing at the boss's jokes. So they decide to trade places for a day. Bea heads to the office in her father's coat and tie, and Mr. Jones heads to Miss Seymour's classroom with a note excusing Bea from class.

The story is a comic role reversal in an ad agency and a kindergarten. Janice Prindle wrote in the Village Voice that "best of all are the pictures." Kenneth Marantz, in School Library Journal, called the story "efficiently trimmed and noncondescending." Publishers Weekly was less enthusiastic, writing that the author's "talent for satire" "goes overboard in burlesquing the characters."

It suits listeners who enjoy a silly role reversal. Betsy Bird, on School Library Journal's Fuse 8 blog, likened it to "Freaky Friday," except that both characters know exactly what is going on.

## Author and illustrator
Amy Schwartz wrote and illustrated this book herself. She was born in San Diego on April 2, 1954, and grew up in Southern California, the daughter of a writer and a chemistry professor. She earned a B.F.A. in drawing from the California College of Arts and Crafts in 1976. A friend introduced her to children's book illustration, and she moved to New York. Editors there suggested she write a story to go with her pictures, so she took writing and illustration classes. *Bea and Mr. Jones*, published by Bradbury Press in 1982, was her first picture book, and the New York Public Library named it one of its 100 Best Children's Books that year. She died on February 26, 2023.

Her other picture books include *Annabelle Swift, Kindergartner*, about a girl's first days at school; *A Teeny Tiny Baby*; and *Oma and Bobo*, a tribute to her own grandmother. *Mrs. Moskowitz and the Sabbath Candlesticks* won the Sydney Taylor Book Award.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with the host in a TV studio, saying, "Show biz. Sometimes it's not all it's cracked up to be." A crew member hands him the book for the next show. The earlier part of that scene, a run of flubbed takes of a tongue twister, was cut, so a child hears only the end of the joke.

Madeline Kahn reads the book. The reading ends on the book's last lines, which wonder whether the "big kid" getting into the movies at half price, or the very short executive at lunch, might be a child like Bea.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar tries on costumes and roles, children say whom they would most like to be, and he talks about his own acting. A poem about daydreamers is read aloud. A 12-year-old boy who started a library for his small Utah town shows how it works. Children recommend books.
