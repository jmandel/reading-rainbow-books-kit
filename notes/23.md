## About the book

*Hill of Fire* is a true story set in a small Mexican village where, according to one farmer, nothing ever happens. He says so at breakfast and in the field, while his neighbors insist the village has everything it needs: a school, a market and a church bell that rings on Sundays. Then his plow sinks into the ground and the earth begins to growl.

The story is based on the birth of the Parícutin volcano in Mexico in 1943. It first appeared in 1971 from Harper & Row as an "I Can Read" book.

Kirkus Reviews, in 1971, wrote that "the energy is chiefly the volcano's, though Joan Sandin's strong sense of design is manifest throughout," but also faulted the art's handling of Mexican skin tones. The book suits listeners who like real events and big noises, and anyone curious about where mountains come from.

## Author and illustrator

Thomas P. Lewis was born on July 1, 1936, in Mount Vernon, New York, and writes for children. Besides *Hill of Fire*, Goodreads lists *Clipper Ship* (1978), *Mr. Sniff and the Motel Mystery* (1984) and *Frida's Office Day* (1989). Joan Sandin illustrated both *Hill of Fire* and *Clipper Ship*.

Joan Sandin was born on April 30, 1942, in Watertown, Wisconsin, and earned a B.F.A. from the University of Arizona in 1964. She is an illustrator, author and translator who lived in Sweden for more than a decade before returning to the United States in the mid-1980s, and she now lives in Tucson, Arizona. Encyclopedia.com lists her illustrating books from 1968 onward and translating from Swedish, including picture books by Olof and Lena Landström.

*Hill of Fire* received a 1971 Notable Children's Trade Book in Social Studies citation from the National Council for the Social Studies and the Children's Book Council. For a next book, Sandin wrote and illustrated *The Long Way to a New Land* (1981) and *Pioneer Bear* (1995), both well-researched historical stories.

## In this episode

The audio opens with a narrator over footage of Hawaii's volcanoes (not shown in the podcast): "Lava is really hot, melted rock that's pumped up 40 miles through tunnels inside the earth." It then introduces this true story, set in Mexico about 45 years before the broadcast. Lines such as "watch how fast it moves!" refer to pictures a listener cannot see.

Fernando Escandon reads the book, ending on the farmer's line, "One hill of fire is enough for me."

Not in the podcast audio: on Kilauea, lava trees and a seismometer, a diagram of the earth's layers, two kinds of lava, a potter at Kilauea's summit who shapes raku vessels inspired by the volcano, three young reviewers, and a geologist at Hawaii Volcanoes National Park who explains the ongoing eruption.

Content note: the audio includes a volcanic eruption, fire, a village partly destroyed and families leaving their homes.
