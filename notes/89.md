## About the book
A small boy tells the story of how he came to his family. His new mother and father have sent him pictures: of themselves, of their red dog, of a white house with a green tree out front, and of a room and a bed just for him. He flies a day and a night to reach them. The first turn comes on the plane, where everything is new and he cannot sleep. Kirkus opens its review by saying that "an adopted preschooler takes a turn at telling a beloved family story," and the book keeps that feeling of a story already told many times, with the boy telling it to his mama.

It suits young listeners who are adopted, know someone who is, or who simply like hearing a quiet, steady tale about night, travel and arriving somewhere safe. Kirkus called the text "quiet, gracefully cadenced" and the illustrations "warm, understated." Horn Book contributor Ellen Fader found that the story "illuminates in a lyrical and compassionate way" the adjustment a child from a faraway country must make.

## Author and illustrator
Ann Turner was born Ann Warren on December 10, 1945, in Northampton, Massachusetts, to a printer father and an artist mother. She studied at Bates College and the University of Massachusetts, taught high school English in Great Barrington, and became a writer in 1971. Her work includes picture books, novels, nonfiction and poetry. She has said her childhood gave her an interest in different people and cultures, and that her editor, Charlotte Zolotow, helped her develop as a writer through this book, an early picture book of hers. The Encyclopedia.com entry lists it as a National Council for the Social Studies Notable Book in 1990.

James Graham Hale illustrated this book for Harper in 1990. Kirkus called him a "new illustrator."

For listeners who want more from Turner, her picture books include *Dakota Dugout* (1985) and *Nettie's Trip South* (1987), both illustrated by Ronald Himler, and *Heron Street* (1989), illustrated by Lisa Desimini.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar at family skate day in Prospect Park: "Hi. It's family skate day at Prospect Park." Kids and parents talk about what annoys them and what they love about their families. LeVar then introduces the book as a story about parents who wanted a child finding a child who needed parents. Andrew Lei reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line.

Content note: the story includes a child's fear, separation from familiar people and a frightening night flight, all resolved gently.

Not in the podcast audio, and cut from the TV episode, are the segments after the reading. LeVar explains adoption. One family retells how their two sons joined them. Another family, with nine adopted children, talks about a mother's love and birth parents. A third family welcomes their adopted baby, Tess.
