## About the book
*Visiting Day* follows a young girl through the one day each month that shapes her life. Chicken is frying in the kitchen at six in the morning, her grandmother is humming, and the two of them set off by bus to see her father, who is in prison. The story is told in the girl's own voice, and the visit itself is a celebration, with peppermints, braids, and a father proud to show off his girl.

The book suits children ages five to eight, according to Kirkus, especially families who know someone who is away. Kirkus (September 15, 2002) described a sensitive approach to a difficult subject and a loving family holding up through hardship, and said it would prompt discussion. The same review plainly noted two reservations: the book never says what the father did, and the racial makeup of the prison scenes, where nearly all inmates and visitors are Black and the guard is white, raises the question of stereotypes. Scholastic Press published it.

## Author and illustrator
Jacqueline Woodson was born on February 12, 1963, in Columbus, Ohio, lived in Ohio and South Carolina as a child, and moved to Brooklyn at about age seven. A children's writing class at The New School led an editor at Delacorte Press to ask for her manuscript, which became her first book, *Last Summer with Maizon* (1990). She won the National Book Award for *Brown Girl Dreaming* and a Newbery Honor for *Show Way*, and was National Ambassador for Young People's Literature in 2018 and 2019. Young listeners might also enjoy her picture book *Each Kindness*.

James E. Ransome was born on September 25, 1961, in Rich Square, North Carolina, and studied at the Pratt Institute in Brooklyn, where illustrator Jerry Pinkney was his mentor. He has illustrated more than 60 children's books. He won the Coretta Scott King Award for illustrator for *The Creation* and an honor for *Uncle Jed's Barbershop*. He lives in Rhinebeck, New York, with his wife, author Lesa Cline-Ransome.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar's lead-in: "Families are meant to be together." He explains that some children are separated from a parent because of divorce or death, and that others have a parent in prison, who can only be visited on certain days. He ends with "This is her story. It's called Visiting Day." Alfre Woodard then reads the whole book, which ends with the girl, her grandmother and the plans they make for when daddy comes home again.

Content note: the audio mentions a parent in prison, missing a father, and feeling a little sad.

Not in the podcast audio: LeVar's remarks after the book, then a visit with the Gooden family. Brothers Malcolm and Malik, their mother, and their father Hopeton Gooden, who is in Sing Sing prison, talk about life without him, the long trip, the security check at the prison, and a birthday shirt. Three young reviewers then share books.
