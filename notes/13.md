## About the book
Arthur is an aardvark who cannot see the board, holds his book so close that his nose gets in the way, and gets headaches. Francine reads the problems to him, and nobody wants to play with him. Then he is fitted for glasses at the optometrist, and he likes how they look. The next morning his friends laugh and call him "four eyes," and Arthur decides the glasses have to go.

The story is a funny look at a feeling many small children know: not wanting to look different from friends. It suits families preparing for a first pair of glasses.

Kirkus Reviews called it "Executed with Brown's customary brash comic energy," but also found the problem of glasses an old story with "many predecessors" and said the telling makes it "less than usually that of a sensitive odd-child-out." The mix-up at the wrong bathroom door is the funniest turn, and Francine has a surprise of her own near the end.

## Author and illustrator
Marc Brown wrote and illustrated *Arthur's Eyes*. He was born on November 25, 1946, in Erie, Pennsylvania, where his father worked on the railroad. A high school art teacher, Nancy Bryan, took him to the Cleveland Institute of Art for an interview, and he received a full scholarship. He graduated in 1968 with a degree in graphic design, and in 1970 he illustrated his first book, *What Makes the Sun Shine?* by Isaac Asimov.

Arthur came from a bedtime story: after several unsuccessful jobs, Brown invented an aardvark for a story his young son had requested. *Arthur's Nose* (1976) was his first picture book. Characters drew on people from his life: Mr. Ratburn was based on his seventh-grade algebra teacher, and Buster on his best friend, Terry Johnson.

Listeners who enjoy his books can look for *One, Two, Three: An Animal Counting Book*, *Moose and Goose* and *Lenny and Lola*. He was an executive producer of the *Arthur* television series for seasons ten through twenty-five and is a three-time Emmy award winner.

## In this episode
The podcast opens on the host's words right after a close-up guessing game: "You've been looking through a very special camera lens that lets you see ordinary things... in a brand new way." That refers to close-up pictures of an orange, broccoli, a potato and an onion, which are cut, so a child hears the line without that context.

Bill Cosby reads *Arthur's Eyes*. The audio ends as Francine takes her movie star glasses out of her purse for the class photograph.

Content note: Arthur is teased, feels awful, and hides his glasses.

Not in the podcast audio: a cold-open game of arguing over close-up pictures from a book of eye riddles; a color-blindness test and poems about skin color; a flip book; young filmmakers drawing to music; a Braille picture book; a mime; and a toy store visit in La Mesa, California.
