## About the book
An old man lives alone in a wide, windswept place "near nowhere and close to forgotten," where his only neighbors are other people's garbage and bad weather. Every day he sorts and burns and buries the rubbish. Every night he dreams of a forest full of wild animals: toucans, tree frogs, tigers. Each morning the world outside is the same. Then one day something catches his eye, and an idea starts to grow.

The text is spare and lyrical, which suits listeners who like a quiet, steady story with a single clear thread. Publishers Weekly called the illustrations "exquisitely detailed," and Kirkus praised Wayne Anderson's detailed art, noting how the dingy blue of the junk gives way to brighter colors. Publishers Weekly described the message as inspiring in its understatement: one person who dares to dream can make a difference. Dutton published it in 2001; Kirkus lists it for ages 6 to 8.

## Author and illustrator
Helen Ward studied at Brighton School of Art, where her teachers included Raymond Briggs, and she was the first recipient of the Walker Prize for Children's Illustration. Pan Macmillan notes that her work won the National Art Library Award for *The Tin Forest*, and that *The Cockerel and the Fox* was shortlisted for the Kate Greenaway Award in 2003. She lives in Stroud, Gloucestershire. Other picture books she has written include *Old Shell, New Shell*, *The Rooster and the Fox* and *The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse*.

Wayne Anderson enrolled at Leicester College of Art in 1961 at age fifteen. His illustration career began in London, and in 1969 he completed his first color commission in colored crayon pencils, the medium he has used for nearly all his color work since. A Château de Belcastel artist biography says he has written or illustrated more than 160 books over fifty-five years, best known for fantasy with dragons and other creatures.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton's lead-in: "Here's a book that's all about the message of hope." He says it is about a man in a dark and gloomy place. Jeff Bridges reads the story. The audio ends on the book's last line, about the old man who "never stopped dreaming."

The lead-in refers to cut material: the gloomy place is "how this neighborhood seemed right after the tragedy." In the TV episode that is lower Manhattan after September 11, 2001, so a child hears "tragedy" without context. Content note: the old man's heart "ached with emptiness" when a visitor leaves.

Not in the podcast audio: a visit to ground zero, then to PS 234, a nearby school whose students spent months in another building. They talk with LeVar about that day and the gifts they received, thank the firefighters and shopkeepers who helped, and make a mural and a thank-you song. Three students review books.
