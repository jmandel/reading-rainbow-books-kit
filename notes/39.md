## About the book
A restaurant owner loves to cook and serve, and works from morning until night. Then a new highway is built nearby, travelers drive straight past, and his tables stay empty. One evening a stranger in worn clothes, with an unusual, gentle manner, comes in. He has no money, but the owner invites him to sit and cooks the best meal he can. To say thank you, the stranger folds a paper napkin into a crane and tells the owner that if he claps his hands, the bird will come to life and dance.

It suits listeners who like a little magic in an everyday place, sparked by a kind gesture. Publishers Weekly called it "amazingly ingenious" and described the pictures as color artwork in which "one sees everything in three-dimensions," an effect "created by lovely collages and paintings." In the same review, a sandwich on a plate and roses in a vase "seem real enough to pick up."

## Author and illustrator
Molly Bang wrote and illustrated *The Paper Crane*. She was born in Princeton, New Jersey, on December 29, 1943. Her mother, Betsy Bang, was an illustrator and her father, Fred Bang, a medical researcher. She attended Wellesley College and earned a master's degree from the University of Arizona in 1969.

Bang began writing children's books after a failed stint as a reporter for *The Baltimore Sun*. She started out illustrating folk tales, including a 1973 collection of Japanese tales, *Men From the Village Deep in the Mountains and Other Japanese Folk Tales*, and later turned to her own stories.

When Publishers Weekly reviewed *The Paper Crane* in 1985, it noted that she had two Caldecott Honors, for *Ten, Nine, Eight* and *The Grey Lady and the Strawberry Snatcher*. *The Paper Crane* then appears on the Boston Globe-Horn Book Award list under 1986 as the picture book winner. Her third Caldecott Honor came later, for *When Sophie Gets Angry, Really, Really Angry...* (1999).

Young listeners might look next for *Ten, Nine, Eight* or *Goose*, also by Bang.

## In this episode
The audio opens with LeVar's lead-in, "One of the most enjoyable parts of Japanese culture is the food." He describes a chef, Mr. Yabafuchi, carving a white radish into a Japanese crane, a bird believed to bring long life and good health. He then introduces "The Paper Crane," by Molly Bang. The lead-in refers to Japanese food and the chef's carving, and the earlier part of the episode is not in the audio, so a child hears it without that context.

Keye Luke reads the book. The audio ends on its last line.
