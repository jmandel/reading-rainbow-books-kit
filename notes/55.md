## About the book
Mufaro lives in a village in Africa with his two daughters, Nyasha and Manyara. Nyasha is gentle and welcomes a small garden snake she calls Nyoka. Manyara is almost always in a bad temper, teases her sister and insists that one day she will be a queen. Then a messenger arrives: the most worthy and beautiful daughters of the land are invited to appear before the king, who will choose one to become his queen. Both sisters set out, and on the way each meets strangers on the path. How each of them treats them shapes everything that follows.

Kirkus Reviews described the story as resetting "the classic Cinderella pattern in an ancient Zimbabwe city," and wrote of the illustrations that "Meticulous crosshatching defines his sculptural forms." The clear contrast between a kind sister and a boastful one, and the snake, the laughing trees and the hungry boy on the path, make this a good fit for listeners who enjoy a fairy tale told aloud.

## Author and illustrator
John Steptoe wrote and illustrated the book. He was born in Brooklyn in 1950, grew up in Bedford-Stuyvesant and attended the High School of Art and Design in Manhattan. He began his first picture book, *Stevie and Me*, at sixteen, and at eighteen published *Stevie* in 1969; *Life* magazine printed that book in full and called it "a new kind of book for black children."

*Mufaro's Beautiful Daughters* grew from a folk tale in an 1895 collection. Writing it led Steptoe to study African culture more deeply than before, and his illustrations draw on his study of an ancient city in Zimbabwe. The book earned a Caldecott Honor and the Coretta Scott King Award for illustration. His daughter Bweela was one of the models for the paintings, and his son Javaka Steptoe later became an author and illustrator too.

Steptoe died in 1989, the year this episode aired. Young listeners could look for his *Stevie* and *The Story of Jumping Mouse*.

## In this episode
The podcast opens with LeVar Burton at a festival in New York's Central Park, where people are singing, dancing and celebrating the music and culture of Africa. He says all Black Americans have roots in Africa, that storytelling is one of his favorite African traditions, and introduces this story as one of the ones he loves best. The festival footage is cut.

Phylicia Rashad reads the book, and the audio ends on its last line about Manyara as a servant in the queen's household.

Content note: Manyara is rude to a hungry boy and an old woman, and later runs back in tears, terrified of a "monster" snake with five heads.

Not in the podcast audio: an African drum maker carving and finishing a djembe drum; a master musician showing instruments such as a conch shell, a gourd trumpet and group percussion from Ghana; the book reviews; and a dance by the group Forces of Nature.
