# The Tin Forest

## About the book

*The Tin Forest* begins in "a wide windswept place near nowhere and close to forgotten," a dumping ground filled with things no one wanted. In a small house with small windows lives an old man who sorts and burns the garbage every day. Every night he dreams of a forest full of wild animals. Then one day something catches his eye, and an idea takes root in his mind.

Reviewers focus on the pictures. *Kirkus Reviews* says Anderson "faithfully portrays every leaf and vine, every nick and rivet," with the junk in dingy blues and the living creatures in brighter hues. *Publishers Weekly* calls the "exquisitely detailed illustrations" touching, and says the bleak landscape has "a jarring, unexpected beauty." *Kirkus* lists the book for ages 6 to 8; *Publishers Weekly* suggests ages 6 to 10. Booklist, as quoted by the publisher, called it "infused with natural wonder," "beautifully illustrated," and "stunning."

## Author and illustrator

Helen Ward and Wayne Anderson both live in England. Their publisher says the two have "worked together on a number of splendid picture books," and *The Tin Forest* is the only title on the publisher's Wayne Anderson page. A *Kirkus* reviewer compared the book to Colin Thompson's *Paper Bag Prince* (1992), a similarly themed story that a listener who enjoys this one might like to find next.

## In this episode

The host's lead-in comes before the title card: "Here's a book that's all about the message of hope. It's about a man who lives in a dark and gloomy place, which is how this neighborhood seemed right after the tragedy. But he has hope in his heart and he discovers that that hope changes his world. His story is found in 'The Tin Forest.'" The book is read by Jeff Bridges.

The cut parts, which are not in the podcast audio, are two field segments. The first opens on a street in lower Manhattan near the World Trade Center site and talks about the September 11 attacks and the neighborhood's recovery. The second follows the students of PS 234 as they talk about that day, their months at another school, and the gifts they received from around the world. The full episode then closes with a review of three books and a song from the students. The podcast audio contains only the lead-in and the book; the lead-in mentions "the tragedy" in general terms and does not describe the attacks.
